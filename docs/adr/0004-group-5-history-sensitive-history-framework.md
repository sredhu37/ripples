# ADR 0004: Group 5 (History) Sensitive History Framework and Curriculum

## Status

Accepted

## Context

Group 5 of Ripples introduces global and regional historical turning points to an 8-year-old child across lessons 35 to 42:

1. Mughals (`mughals`)
2. Spice Trade (`spicetrade`)
3. British in India (`britishinindia`)
4. Independence (`independence`)
5. World War 1 (`ww1`)
6. World War 2 (`ww2`)
7. Rebuilding Europe (`rebuilding`)
8. Why History Matters (`whyhistory`)

Teaching world history, colonization, and major wars to young children carries significant pedagogical and psychological risks:

1. Trauma and fear: Depicting graphic battlefield violence, casualties, or atrocities can frighten a young child and shut down curious learning.
2. Cartoon villainy and racial caricaturing: Reducing complex empires, nations, and trade companies to moralistic slogans prevents children from understanding economic incentives, institutional structures, and political dynamics.
3. Cynicism and helplessness: Presenting history merely as a sequence of unending cruelty can foster cynicism rather than civic responsibility.
4. Nationalist bias: Glorifying conquest or erasing historical realities harms balanced critical thinking. In the context of Ripples (designed for children bridging Indian heritage and German/European life), the lessons must weave Indian and European history with factual accuracy, mutual respect, and profound faith in democratic reconciliation.

To prepare for implementation in task t_dd09293e (GitHub issue #29), this ADR establishes the sensitive history framework, pedagogical guardrails, and complete lesson specifications for Group 5.

## Decision

We establish four pedagogical principles, articulate tone and boundary standards for sensitive history, and specify the full 8-lesson curriculum blueprint.

### 1. The Four Pedagogical Principles

#### Principle A: Mechanisms and Incentives Over Graphic Violence

History is driven by institutional designs, trade monopolies, resource flows, alliance obligations, and propaganda.

- We explain **why** events happened through concrete economic and governance mechanics: tariffs, exclusive royal charters, trade choke-points, alliance tripwires, and war bonds.
- We strictly omit gruesome battlefield gore, physical injury, and traumatic imagery. Conflict is visualized through maps, ships, trade routes, alliance webs, broken treaty scrolls, and ruined bridges, paired with constructive tools of rebuilding.

#### Principle B: People vs. Rulers and Regimes

A critical rule of historical literacy is distinguishing between ordinary people and authoritarian rulers or corporate monopolies.

- In `britishinindia`, we distinguish between the private monopoly chartered East India Company (motivated by shareholder profits and extracting cotton/spices) and ordinary British or Indian citizens.
- In `ww2`, we distinguish between the criminal totalitarian Nazi regime and everyday people, highlighting civilian courage and resistance (such as the White Rose / Weiße Rose).

#### Principle C: Non-Violent Agency as the True Superpower

Wars and conquests are not presented as heroic triumphs of force. True lasting progress is achieved through moral courage, intellectual reform, and non-violent civic action.

- In `mughals`, we emphasize Emperor Akbar's _Ibadat Khana_ (hall of interfaith dialogue), administrative efficiency, and shared cultural synthesis (architecture, astronomy, miniature art).
- In `independence`, Mahatma Gandhi's _Satyagraha_ (truth force), the Salt March, and the hand-spun cloth (_Khadi_) movement demonstrate how millions of ordinary citizens won independence without weapons.

#### Principle D: The Architecture of Reconciliation

Every conflict lesson leads directly to the constructive institutions built to prevent its recurrence.

- World War 1 leads to the search for collective diplomacy (League of Nations / Voelkerbund).
- World War 2 leads directly to the founding of the United Nations (Charta der Vereinten Nationen) and the universal declaration of human rights.
- Devastated post-war Europe leads to the European Coal and Steel Community (EGKS) and Franco-German friendship (Elysee Treaty), where former enemies shared coal and steel so war became materially impossible.

### 3. Curriculum Blueprint for Group 5

#### Lesson 35: The Mughals (`mughals`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach how the Mughal Empire united diverse regions across South Asia through administrative systems, cultural synthesis, and religious tolerance under Emperor Akbar.
- Narrative: In the 16th century, Emperor Akbar rules a vast land of mountains, river valleys, and bustling trade towns. Akbar realizes an empire cannot flourish through force alone. He builds the _Ibadat Khana_ (House of Wisdom) where scholars of Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, Jainism, and Zoroastrianism discuss ethics and truth. Under Akbar and his successors, grand roads (Grand Trunk Road), astronomical observatories, magnificent stone architecture (like the Taj Mahal and Red Fort), and fine textiles make South Asia one of the richest manufacturing regions on Earth.
- Starred Terms: _empire_ / _Grossreich_, _synthesis_ / _Verschmelzung_, _tolerance_ / _Toleranz_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - Dialogue Table: Four scholars representing diverse traditions sit around Akbar's council table.
  - The child selects topics (Justice, Road Building, Fair Taxes, Arts) and watches harmony meters rise.
  - If a player tries "Only My Way", harmony drops and merchants leave; selecting "Listen & Learn" raises trade and cultural flourishing.
- Quiz Widget: Place all four scholars at the council table to achieve imperial harmony.
- Real Life: The Humboldt Forum in Berlin and the British Museum preserve Mughal miniature paintings, intricate carpets, and astronomical astrolabes.
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. Irfan Habib, _The Agrarian System of Mughal India_, Oxford University Press.
  2. Audrey Truschke, _Akbar: The Great Emperor of India_, Penguin Books.
  3. National Museum New Delhi, Mughal decorative arts and coin archives.

#### Lesson 36: The Spice Trade (`spicetrade`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach how the European desire for black pepper, cinnamon, and nutmeg drove maritime navigation, connecting continents while introducing corporate trade monopolies.
- Narrative: Spices like black pepper and nutmeg once cost more than silver in European markets because camel caravans took years to cross the Silk Road. Navigators like Vasco da Gama set sail around Africa, guided by monsoon winds and Arab ocean charts. European merchants formed the world's first joint-stock corporations (the Dutch VOC and the English East India Company) to fund fleets of trading ships. While spices brought new flavors and global connections, the monopoly companies used fortresses and naval cannons to lock out local merchants.
- Starred Terms: _spice trade_ / _Gewuerzhandel_, _monsoon_ / _Monsun_, _monopoly_ / _Monopol_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - Spice Caravan vs. Caravel: Trade route map from Calicut (Kerala) to Venice/Hamburg.
  - Land route takes 12 months with high toll fees; ocean route takes 4 months using monsoon wind sliders (Summer South-West vs. Winter North-East).
  - Child adjusts sail angle to catch seasonal winds and safely transport pepper sacks to the harbor.
- Quiz Widget: Catch the summer monsoon wind to navigate the caravel from the coast of Malabar across the Arabian Sea.
- Real Life: Hamburg's historic _Speicherstadt_ (now a UNESCO World Heritage site) was built as the world's largest warehouse complex for spices, coffee, and cocoa imported by sailing fleets.
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. K. N. Chaudhuri, _Trade and Civilisation in the Indian Ocean_, Cambridge University Press.
  2. UNESCO World Heritage Centre, _Speicherstadt and Kontorhaus District with Chilehaus_.
  3. Deutsches Schiffahrtsmuseum Bremerhaven, historical navigation instruments.

#### Lesson 37: The British in India (`britishinindia`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach how a private chartered corporation (the East India Company) gradually took control of Indian taxation and trade, transforming a leading textile manufacturing hub into an exporter of raw materials.
- Narrative: In 1600, Queen Elizabeth I granted a royal charter to a private business: the East India Company. The Company initially came only to trade, but soon hired private armies and built stone forts. Following the Battle of Plassey (1757), the Company gained the right to collect taxes (_Diwani_) in Bengal. Instead of buying handloom cotton fabrics, British industrial steam looms in Manchester flooded India with machine-made cloth, while raw Indian cotton and tea were shipped away on new railways built for export. In 1858, following widespread uprisings, the British Crown took direct control, establishing the British Raj.
- Starred Terms: _chartered company_ / _Handelskompanie_, _extraction_ / _Rohstoffexport_, _raj_ / _Kolonialherrschaft_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - The Handloom vs. Steam Engine Balance:
  - Left side: Bengal artisan handloom making fine muslin. Right side: Manchester steam factory.
  - When the Company imposes high export tariffs on Indian cloth and zero tariffs on British machine yarn, the local village handloom falls silent.
  - Child adjusts tariff levers to observe how colonial trade policies shifted manufacturing wealth.
- Quiz Widget: Rebalance trade by removing the unfair colonial tariff so village weavers can sell their cotton cloth freely.
- Real Life: The Victoria Memorial in Kolkata and the German Historical Museum in Berlin exhibit colonial trade ledgers and handloom textiles.
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. Shashi Tharoor, _Inglorious Empire: What the British Did to India_, Penguin Random House.
  2. Tirthankar Roy, _The Economic History of India 1857-1947_, Oxford University Press.
  3. William Dalrymple, _The Anarchy: The Relentless Rise of the East India Company_, Bloomsbury.

#### Lesson 38: Independence and Non-Violence (`independence`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach how India won independence in 1947 through non-violent mass protest, civil disobedience (_Satyagraha_), and moral courage.
- Narrative: Millions of Indian citizens wanted self-rule (_Swaraj_). Mahatma Gandhi proposed a revolutionary method: _Ahimsa_ (non-injury) and _Satyagraha_ (insistence on truth). In 1930, when the British government banned Indians from making their own salt and imposed a heavy salt tax, Gandhi walked 240 miles to the sea at Dandi. He knelt down, picked up a lump of natural sea salt, and inspired millions to defy the law peacefully. Citizens boycotted foreign cloth by spinning their own thread on wooden wheels (_Charkha_). On August 15, 1947, India became a free, independent democracy, led by Jawaharlal Nehru and framed by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar's constitution guaranteeing equal rights to all citizens.
- Starred Terms: _independence_ / _Unabhaengigkeit_, _non-violence_ / _Gewaltfreiheit_, _constitution_ / _Verfassung_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - The Spinning Wheel (_Charkha_) & Salt March:
  - Interactive spinning wheel: child turns the wheel to spin raw cotton into honest homespun yarn.
  - As yarn builds up, community solidarity meters fill up peacefully, showing how self-reliance and non-violent unity overcame military dominance.
- Quiz Widget: Spin the Charkha to produce three spools of homespun thread and complete the peaceful salt protest.
- Real Life: In Germany, Mahatma Gandhi's philosophy of non-violent resistance inspired the peaceful Monday demonstrations (_Montagsdemonstrationen_) in Leipzig in 1989, which brought down the Berlin Wall without a single shot fired.
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. B. R. Nanda, _Mahatma Gandhi: A Biography_, Oxford University Press.
  2. Constitution of India, Preamble and Fundamental Rights (Ministry of Law and Justice).
  3. Bundesarchiv, _Die friedliche Revolution im Herbst 1989_.

#### Lesson 39: World War 1 and Alliance Traps (`ww1`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach how secret alliances, militarism, and diplomatic failures dragged European nations into World War 1, leading to the creation of the League of Nations.
- Narrative: In 1914, Europe was split into two giant alliance blocs: the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary) and the Allied Powers (France, Britain, Russia). Leaders built huge armies and battle fleets, believing alliances would keep peace. But alliances became a dangerous domino chain: when an Austrian archduke was assassinated in Sarajevo, mutual defense treaties triggered automatic mobilizations within weeks. Millions of young soldiers marched into industrial warfare with machine guns and trenches. When fighting ended on November 11, 1918, four great empires had collapsed. Nations founded the League of Nations (_Voelkerbund_), realizing that secret alliance traps must be replaced by open international dialogue.
- Starred Terms: _alliance_ / _Buendnis_, _mobilization_ / _Mobilmachung_, _league of nations_ / _Voelkerbund_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - The Alliance Domino Board:
  - Six European nations connected by treaty cords.
  - If cords are rigid tripwires, one incident topples all six dominoes into war.
  - Child replaces rigid military tripwires with the "Diplomatic Mediation Table" where disputes are resolved through open talks, preventing the domino cascade.
- Quiz Widget: Unlink the secret military tripwire and connect both sides to the Geneva Peace Table.
- Real Life: The Peace Palace (_Vredespaleis_) in The Hague, Netherlands, houses the Permanent Court of Arbitration and the International Court of Justice, established to settle border and treaty disputes through law rather than war.
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. Christopher Clark, _The Sleepwalkers: How Europe Went to War in 1914_, HarperCollins.
  2. Treaty of Versailles (1919), Covenant of the League of Nations.
  3. Deutsches Historisches Museum (DHM) Berlin, _Der Erste Weltkrieg 1914-1918_.

#### Lesson 40: World War 2 and the Human Cost (`ww2`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach how the rise of dictatorship in Germany unleashed World War 2, emphasizing the devastation of total war, civil courage against tyranny, and the imperative of human dignity.
- Narrative: In the 1930s, Adolf Hitler and the Nazi party established a brutal dictatorship in Germany, destroying democratic elections and spreading aggressive propaganda. In September 1939, Germany invaded Poland, igniting World War 2 across Europe and Asia. The war brought unimaginable destruction to cities, homes, and innocent lives. Yet even in the darkest times, brave individuals showed extraordinary civil courage: students Sophie and Hans Scholl of the White Rose (_Weisse Rose_) distributed leaflets in Munich urging resistance against tyranny. In 1945, the dictatorship fell, leaving cities in rubble. The world resolved: "Never again" (_Nie wieder_), founding the United Nations and enshrining human rights into international law.
- Starred Terms: _propaganda_ / _Propaganda_, _civil courage_ / _Zivilcourage_, _human rights_ / _Menschenrechte_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - The Defense of Human Rights:
  - In the center sits the Universal Declaration of Human Rights / Grundgesetz Article 1 ("Human dignity shall be inviolable").
  - Dark clouds of "Censorship", "Hate", and "Dictatorship" drift in; the child activates pillars of "Free Speech", "Equal Rights", and "Courage" to protect the flame of liberty.
- Quiz Widget: Place the pillar of "Human Dignity" to illuminate the city and banish censorship.
- Real Life: In Germany, bronze _Stolpersteine_ (stumbling stones) set into sidewalks outside former homes commemorate victims of the Nazi regime, while the German Basic Law (_Grundgesetz_) opens with Article 1: "Die Wuerde des Menschen ist unantastbar" (Human dignity shall be inviolable).
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. German Basic Law (_Grundgesetz_), Article 1 and Article 20.
  2. United Nations, _Universal Declaration of Human Rights_ (1948).
  3. White Rose Foundation Munich (_Weisse Rose Stiftung e.V._), leaflet archives.

#### Lesson 41: Rebuilding Europe (`rebuilding`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach how war-torn European nations replaced centuries of rivalry with economic and political cooperation through the European Coal and Steel Community and Franco-German friendship.
- Narrative: In 1945, Europe's bridges, factories, and railway tracks lay in ruins. Instead of punishing defeated nations with crippling reparations, visionaries like Robert Schuman, Jean Monnet, and Konrad Adenauer proposed a brilliant economic solution. In 1951, France, Germany, Italy, and the Benelux nations placed their entire coal and steel production under a joint High Authority: the European Coal and Steel Community (_EGKS_). Since weapons could not be built without coal and steel, pooling these raw materials made war between France and Germany materially impossible. In 1963, Charles de Gaulle and Konrad Adenauer signed the Elysee Treaty, turning bitter historical enemies into closest friends. This foundation grew into the European Union.
- Starred Terms: _rebuilding_ / _Wiederaufbau_, _reconciliation_ / _Versoehnung_, _european union_ / _Europaeische Union_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - The Coal & Steel Bridge:
  - A broken bridge between two shores (France and Germany).
  - Rather than building stone border walls or cannons, the child takes shared coal tokens and steel beams from a central European pool to rebuild the arched railway bridge.
  - When the bridge connects, trains carrying food, books, and students travel freely between both nations.
- Quiz Widget: Allocate shared steel beams to complete the peace bridge across the river border.
- Real Life: The European Union and the Schengen Agreement allow families to travel seamlessly between Germany, France, and neighboring nations without passport checks or border guards.
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. The Schuman Declaration (9 May 1950), European Commission historical archives.
  2. The Elysee Treaty (22 January 1963), German-French Cooperation Treaty.
  3. European Union, _The History of the European Union: 1945-1959_.

#### Lesson 42: Why History Matters (`whyhistory`)

- Pedagogical Goal: Teach why societies remember the past, examine primary sources, and practice memorial culture to protect democracy and build a compassionate future.
- Narrative: Why do we spend time learning about events that happened decades or centuries ago? Because the world we live in today was built by the decisions of people in the past. When we study history, we look at primary evidence: old letters, treaties, stone inscriptions, and photographs. We learn from mistakes so we do not repeat them, and we celebrate courage so we know how to defend freedom today. In Germany, citizens practice _Erinnerungskultur_ (memorial culture), believing that an honest look at history is the strongest foundation for peace.
- Starred Terms: _primary source_ / _Primaerquelle_, _memory culture_ / _Erinnerungskultur_, _future_ / _Zukunft_.
- Toy Mechanics:
  - The Historian's Archive & Telescope:
  - The child examines historical clues: a clay seal from Mohenjo-daro, a Mughal astrolabe, a spool of homespun cotton, a broken WW1 telegraph, and a 1951 coal-and-steel treaty token.
  - Matching each clue to its historical lesson lights up the "Beacon of Democratic Memory", showing how the past guides future choices.
- Quiz Widget: Place the primary source document on the archive podium to verify the historical lesson.
- Real Life: Across Germany and Europe, archives, municipal museums, and commemorative monuments help schoolchildren study local history and understand how democracy was built.
- Fact Card Citations:
  1. Bundeszentrale fuer politische Bildung (BpB), _Erinnerungskultur in Deutschland_.
  2. International Council of Museums (ICOM), _Code of Ethics for Museums_.
  3. UNESCO, _Recommendation concerning the Protection and Promotion of Museums and Collections_.
